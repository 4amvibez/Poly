## Full narration

How do Japanese people read such a long sentence, without spaces?

In English, spaces tell us where one word ends, and the next begins. But Japanese? Words often run together, without spaces. So how do readers know where one word ends, and another begins?

The first clue is kanji. Look at this sentence.
私は日本語を勉強します。
Kanji carries important meaning. When readers see "nihongo", they can recognize the word for Japanese language.

Next comes hiragana. Characters like "wa", "o", and "shimasu" help readers understand how the sentence is structured. They show grammatical relationships, and how words work together.

And katakana? It often helps readers recognize foreign-origin words, names, and certain emphasized expressions. For example, コーヒー (kōhī, in Japanese) means coffee.

But here's the real secret. Japanese readers don't rely on just one clue. They combine kanji, kana, grammar, vocabulary, and context, to recognize meaningful chunks of a sentence. Their brains learn to see the boundaries, even when spaces aren't written.

At first, Japanese can look like one endless line. But once you understand the patterns, the sentence starts to make sense.

## Japanese on screen (checked)

- 私は日本語を勉強します。 (watashi wa nihongo o benkyō shimasu) "I study Japanese." Chunks 私は | 日本語を | 勉強します。
- は is the topic particle, read "wa"; を is the object particle, read "o"; します is the polite form of する "do"
- 日本語 にほんご nihongo "Japanese language"; 学校 がっこう gakkō "school"; 東京 とうきょう Tōkyō; 電車 でんしゃ densha "train"
- 漢字 kanji, ひらがな hiragana, カタカナ katakana, かな kana; 文法 bunpō "grammar"; 語彙 goi "vocabulary"; 文脈 bunmyaku "context"; 秘密 himitsu "secret"
- パソコン pasokon "computer" (from "personal computer"); ラージ rāji, Raj written in katakana; スゴイ！ sugoi "amazing!" (すごい, written in katakana for emphasis); コーヒー kōhī "coffee" (spoken by the Japanese voice)
- 毎朝コーヒーを飲みます。 (maiasa kōhī o nomimasu) "I drink coffee every morning."; 東京の学校まで電車で行きます。 (Tōkyō no gakkō made densha de ikimasu) "I go to school in Tokyo by train."
- 一歩ずつ (ippo zutsu) "one step at a time"
- Sign-off: 最後まで見てくれてありがとう。またね！ (saigo made mite kurete arigatō. mata ne!). Your note had 最終まで; 最後まで "to the end" is the natural form, and is what the Japanese voice says.
- The hook's handscroll text is the opening of Natsume Sōseki's 吾輩は猫である (1905, public domain).
