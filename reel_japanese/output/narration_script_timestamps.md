## Full narration

Japanese has three scripts. But... why?

Long before it was written down, Japanese was a living, spoken language, carried in conversation, song, and story. Its deeper origins are still debated. But for centuries, it had no script of its own.

Writing came from across the sea. Through centuries of contact with China and the Korean Peninsula, Chinese characters reached the Japanese islands.

These characters, kanji, were designed for Chinese, a very different language. So scribes in Japan adapted them: some for their meaning, others purely for their sound.

Writing sounds with complex characters was slow. As scribes wrote faster, in flowing cursive brushwork, the shapes softened. From these cursive forms came hiragana. The kanji "an", for example, became the hiragana "a". Hiragana flourished in the poetry and literature of the Heian court.

Meanwhile, Buddhist scholars needed quick notes beside Chinese texts. They took just one piece of a character, like the left side of the kanji "i", and created katakana. Sharp, compact, precise.

Today, all three work together. Kanji carry core meanings. Hiragana handle grammar. Katakana mark foreign words, and emphasis.

Take this sentence. Watashi wa nihongo o benkyou shimasu. I study Japanese. Kanji form the core words. Hiragana link them, and complete the verb.

Every line of Japanese carries more than a thousand years of history: borrowed, reshaped, and made its own.

And you can still read it on every street in Japan today. Three scripts. One language. Follow for more Japanese content.
